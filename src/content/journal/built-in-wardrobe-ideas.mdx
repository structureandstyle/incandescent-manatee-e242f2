This guide brings those choices together, from alcove wardrobes and sloping ceilings to door styles, drawer layouts and practical dimensions. Use the ideas to narrow down what suits your room, then develop the details around your clothes, your space and your budget.

<Theme id="layout" count="Layout" title="Start with the bedroom layout" />

Before choosing doors or colours, look at the room with the bed, bedside tables and other furniture in position. Consider the view from the doorway, the route around the bed and the space needed to open cupboards and drawers. A wardrobe needs to work with the whole room.

### A full wall of wardrobes

A continuous run creates generous storage and a clear, ordered frontage. Full-height doors emphasise the room’s height; separate top cupboards introduce a horizontal division and give bedding or luggage their own space.

The door divisions should suit both the room and the storage behind them. A drawer compartment, for example, needs to line up with a door opening that gives access to its full width. Work out the interior and the fronts together.

### Wardrobes either side of a chimney breast

Alcoves are a natural place for bedroom storage, but the recess itself may not be deep enough for clothes on conventional hangers. A hanging wardrobe will often need to project beyond the chimney breast into the room. That projection should be shown in the design, with the exposed sides finished to match the fronts or complement the room.

The two alcoves may differ in width. Matching door details, heights and handles can connect the wardrobes visually while each interior makes use of its own space.

### Storage around the bed

Wardrobes on either side of the bed can combine clothes storage with bedside shelves or drawers. Cupboards above the headboard add space for less frequently used items.

Plan this arrangement around the actual bed and how you use it. Leave comfortable room to sit up against the headboard, reach bedside storage and use reading lights. Overhead cupboards should sit at a deliberate height, with their support and fixings resolved in the design.

### Wardrobes under a sloping ceiling

Use the taller part of the room for hanging clothes and the lower areas for shelves, drawers or cupboards. The position of the wardrobe front matters: a slope can reduce usable depth as well as height.

Check how every door opens beneath the slope and what can be reached through it. A clean arrangement of usable compartments is more valuable than a complicated pattern of small doors following every change in ceiling height.

### Corner wardrobes

An L-shaped run can use two adjoining walls, but the corner needs a planned access arrangement. Clothes, shelves and drawers must remain reachable, and adjacent doors should open without colliding. Sometimes a shorter run on one wall leaves a more comfortable bedroom than wrapping storage around both.

For a quiet, integrated appearance, paint the fronts in the wall colour or a closely related shade. Simple door divisions and restrained handles help the wardrobe sit within the room.

A long run does not always need an open section to break it up. Continuous doors can create a calm background. A dressing table, mirror or display niche makes sense when it has a useful role in the layout.

### Shaker doors

Shaker doors have a flat centre panel surrounded by a simple frame. They give the fronts definition without heavy decoration and work with both period details and contemporary furnishings. A slimmer frame creates a lighter look; wider framing gives the doors more presence.

### Traditional panelled doors

Beads, mouldings and decorative panels create a more detailed frontage. A cornice, suitable handles and carefully chosen panel proportions can connect the wardrobe with existing woodwork. The details should suit the scale of the room rather than compete with it.

### Flat doors

Flat fronts create a simple surface that lets colour, grain and handles do more of the visual work. Visible pulls, recessed finger grips and push-to-open fittings offer different ways to finish the design. If you prefer handleless doors, agree how they will open before choosing the final detailing.

### Decorative and mirrored panels

Reeded details, framed mirrors or fabric-backed panels can add texture and character. Use them selectively to support the room’s design. A mirror is most useful where there is room to stand back and where its reflection is worth seeing.

<Theme id="doors" count="Hinged or sliding" title="Choose hinged or sliding doors" />

Hinged doors suit many bespoke layouts and offer a wide choice of door styles. They can open neighbouring compartments together, provided there is space for the doors to swing. Check the bed, bedside furniture, wall returns and handles against that movement.

Sliding doors are useful where swing space is limited. Because the panels overlap, part of the wardrobe remains covered as another part opens. Position drawers and pull-outs within the available openings, and account for the chosen track system when calculating the overall depth.

Internal drawers need particular attention with either system. Hinged doors and hinges must clear the drawer’s path; sliding doors must move far enough to expose it. The opening mechanism belongs in the interior planning, not at the end of it.

<Theme id="storage" count="Storage" title="Plan storage around your clothes" />

Start by looking inside your current wardrobe. Measure the hanging space you use, separate long garments from shorter ones and count shoes and bags. Note what ends up on a chair or in another room: those items reveal what the new layout needs to solve.

### Hanging space

Double hanging puts two rails within the same compartment for shirts, jackets and folded trousers. Long dresses and coats need a section with a greater clear drop. Measure your longest garments on their hangers so the hems can hang freely above the surface below.

### Shelves

Shelves suit knitwear, folded clothes, bags and storage boxes. Shallower shelves can make individual stacks easier to see and reach, even within a deeper cabinet. Adjustable shelves allow the layout to change; fixed shelves can form part of the cabinet structure.

### Drawers

Drawers keep underwear, socks and smaller items together. Dividers can organise jewellery, watches and accessories, while deeper drawers suit bulkier clothing. Full-extension runners give access to the back of the drawer.

Agree the usable space inside the drawer, not just the height of its front. The drawer base, sides and runner system all affect what fits. Drawers can sit behind the wardrobe doors or form a separate section beneath them, with their own visible fronts. The second option lets you open a drawer without opening a wardrobe door first.

### Shoes and bags

Allow for the footwear you own, including larger shoes and boots. Flat shelves offer flexibility; angled shelves and pull-out racks improve visibility in some layouts. Bags can have individual shelves or shared compartments sized around the collection.

Keep everyday items within comfortable reach. Higher cupboards are useful for seasonal clothes, spare bedding and luggage.

### Internal depth and overall depth

The space available for clothes is smaller than the wardrobe’s projection from the room wall. The doors, back panel, any rear gap and construction clearances all need to be included.

For example, 580 mm clear inside, a 12 mm back, a 20 mm rear gap and a 22 mm door total 634 mm before any additional front clearance. This is an example of the calculation, not a fixed specification. Sliding-door systems need their own depth calculation.

A shallower cabinet can still provide useful folded storage, shoe storage or front-to-back hanging. Select the rail and check the clothes fit before committing to the depth.

### Drawer heights and room clearance

There is no single drawer height that suits every layout. A shallow accessory tray and a drawer for jumpers need different usable heights. Specify the internal space around the contents, then select the drawer construction and runners.

Check circulation with doors and drawers shown open. A gap that allows someone to walk past a closed wardrobe may be too tight to stand and use a drawer comfortably. Include the handles and the actual bed frame in that check.

<Theme id="accessories" count="Accessories" title="Accessories that earn their space" />

Choose accessories around a particular need. A well-positioned rail or shelf can be more useful than a mechanism you rarely use.

| Accessory | What it adds | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Pull-down hanging rail | Brings a high rail within easier reach | Width range, load rating, operating effort and full movement path |
| Pull-out trouser rack | Separates trousers and brings them forward | Depth, handedness, garment drop and door clearance |
| Jewellery or accessory tray | Divided storage for small items | Compartment sizes and usable drawer height |
| Shoe pull-out | Brings footwear into view | Shoe size, rack height and extraction space |
| Laundry basket | Conceals laundry within the layout | Capacity, removal and access |
| Pull-out mirror | Slides out from inside the wardrobe and turns towards you | Depth, turning space and room to stand back; an internal door mirror is another option |
| Valet rail | Temporary hanging for an outfit | Projection and clearance around the user |

Accessory dimensions vary between systems. Select the fitting during the design stage so its compartment provides enough space for installation, movement and comfortable access.

<Theme id="materials" count="Materials" title="Materials and finishes" />

The exterior and interior can use different materials. Painted doors with a decorative-board interior, for example, combine a chosen exterior colour with a ready-finished surface inside.

### Hand-painted MDF

Painted MDF suits flat doors, Shaker detailing and more decorative designs. Hand painting offers a broad choice of colour and an applied finish whose appearance should be agreed from a sample. Preparation, paint system and sheen all influence the result.

### Sprayed MDF

Spraying creates a uniform painted surface across doors and panels. Choose the colour and sheen alongside the room’s other finishes, and inspect a sample of the specified coating. The coating system and preparation determine performance as well as appearance.

### Decorative melamine-faced board

Melamine-faced board offers ready-finished colours, textures and wood effects for interiors and complete wardrobes. Coordinated edging finishes the exposed edges. Physical samples help compare texture and grain with the flooring and other furniture.

### Real wood veneer

Veneer brings real timber grain to doors, panels and interiors. The species, grain direction and chosen clear finish or stain shape the appearance. Agree how neighbouring panels will relate so the grain forms part of the design.

### Choosing colour

View samples in the bedroom, in daylight and under the lighting you use in the evening. Compare them with the floor, curtains and headboard. A pale wardrobe can remain a distinct feature; a deeper colour can integrate with matching walls. The relationship between the finishes matters more than a rule that small rooms must have white furniture.

<Theme id="light" count="Lighting" title="Plan lighting with the joinery" />

Interior lighting helps you see clothes and distinguish colours. Vertical strips near the front of compartments can illuminate hanging garments, while lighting beneath shelves can serve folded storage. Position the fittings so clothes and shelves do not block the light.

Door sensors can switch the lights on automatically. Open display sections may benefit from a separate control so their lighting can be used independently. Agree the light colour using samples alongside the interior finish.

Cable routes, profiles, sensors and the power supply need to be included in the drawings. Keep drivers and connections accessible for maintenance, and choose fittings suitable for their installation.

<Theme id="finishing" count="Finishing" title="Finish the wardrobe into the room" />

### At the ceiling

A plain top infill creates a clean junction with the ceiling. Separate top cupboards give high storage its own doors. A cornice can add a traditional finish or relate to existing mouldings. Choose the arrangement alongside the door proportions so the top of the wardrobe looks deliberate.

### At the walls and floor

Levelling and scribed infills accommodate uneven floors, walls and ceilings. Allow for them in the measured design. Decide how existing skirting, picture rails and cornice will meet the new joinery rather than leaving those junctions to be improvised during fitting.

Check the floor construction before setting out a permanent wardrobe. Where the floor floats, follow its manufacturer’s requirements so the installation allows the required movement.

### Services and wall conditions

Locate sockets, switches, heating pipes and vents before covering the wall. Agree any relocation and preserve access where needed. Investigate existing damp before installing joinery against it. A rear gap may form part of the design, but it does not resolve the cause of a moisture problem.

<Theme id="cost" count="Cost" title="What affects the cost" />

Wardrobe size is only part of the specification. Drawers, specialist pull-outs, lighting, door detailing, materials and finishes all affect the price. Sloping ceilings and awkward access can add design, manufacturing and fitting work.

For a useful comparison, ask for the same height, width, depth and interior layout across material options. Check which surfaces are painted or veneered, how many drawers are included and whether lighting and installation are part of the figure.
